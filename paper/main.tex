\begin{abstract}
Identity verification asks whether a document supports a claim about its holder,
and is usually implemented as string matching. On Indian identity documents the
holder's name is printed twice---in a regional script and in Latin---and neither
matching nor lookup survives contact with real data: romanisation is one-to-many
(97.6\% of Urdu words have more than one human romanisation), and OCR
\emph{deletes} characters far more often than it substitutes them. We recast
verification as three-way document-level natural language inference in which
\neu denotes that the document carries insufficient evidence, and is routed to a
human. We introduce \kyc, a synthetic benchmark of 7,000 instances in
minimal-pair contrast groups across five scripts, with OCR noise calibrated
against a real engine rather than assumed. A transliteration-aware rule engine
is at ceiling on clean input (0.991 macro-F1) but collapses under realistic
noise (0.380); a cross-encoder trained only at moderate noise holds 0.911 on
heavier noise it never saw. Holding the automation error rate fixed, the rule
engine cannot meet a 5\% error budget at any threshold, while the cross-encoder
meets it at its default operating point, deferring only the 28.6\% of cases that
are undecidable by construction. The difference is calibration: the learned
model's confidence ranks its own correctness, and the rule engine's does not.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Know-your-customer (KYC) verification reduces to a sequence of questions of one
form: \emph{does this document support this claim about its holder?} Production
systems answer by comparing strings. That works on a clean scan of a document
written in one script, and it fails in exactly the setting where verification is
most needed.

An Indian passport prints the holder's name twice: once in the Visual
Inspection Zone (VIZ) in a regional script, and once in the Latin-only Machine
Readable Zone (MRZ) mandated by ICAO Doc~9303~\citep{icao9303}. Two independent
problems then arise. First, the mapping from native script to Latin is
\emph{one-to-many}: there is no authority that fixes the spelling of a Hindi,
Tamil or Bengali name, and the same holder may romanise their own name
differently on different forms. Measured over human-written romanisations
(\S\ref{sec:premises}), 57\% of Hindi words and 97.6\% of Urdu words have more
than one accepted spelling. Second, the document is read by OCR, which---we
find---mostly \emph{deletes} characters rather than substituting them. A
substituted character leaves something to match against; a deleted one removes
the evidence entirely.

String matching handles neither, and a lookup table cannot handle the first even
in principle, because there is no single correct answer to look up.
Figure~\ref{fig:task} shows the consequence on the first Hindi identity in our
held-out set: for the true claim that the holder's surname is
\textsc{Chatterjee}, exact matching rejects a genuine customer, a strong
transliteration-aware rule engine abstains and sends the case to a human, and a
learned model decides it correctly.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig1_task}
  \caption{The task on the first Hindi test identity (\texttt{hi-00000}; not
  selected for its outcome). OCR has deleted characters from both zones: the
  VIZ has lost the consonant under its vowel sign (the shaper marks the orphaned

  zone alone settles the claim; together they do. All three predictions are the
  systems' actual outputs.}
  \label{fig:task}

We argue that verification should be posed as \emph{natural language inference}
over the whole document, with a three-way label. \ent and \con are decisions;
\neu is not a low-confidence decision but a statement about the premise---the
document does not contain the evidence needed to adjudicate the claim---and is
the case a deployed system routes to human review. This framing makes the
operational quantity of interest, the share of traffic a human must inspect,
part of the task definition rather than an afterthought.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A task and benchmark.} \kyc frames identity verification as
    document-level NLI over multi-zone, multi-script observations, with
    \neu defined structurally as evidence insufficiency. Its 7,000 instances
    come in minimal-pair contrast groups~\citep{gardner2020contrast} across
    Latin, Devanagari, Tamil, Bengali and Perso-Arabic, with an identity-level
    split that no held-out document shares with training.
  \item \textbf{Measured, not assumed, noise.} We calibrate the OCR noise model
    against Tesseract~\citep{smith2007tesseract} on correctly shaped renderings
    of all five scripts. A hand-built model of the kind common in robustness
    work had the error distribution backwards, and correcting it moved a strong
    baseline from 0.831 to 0.597 macro-F1.
  \item \textbf{A deliberately strong baseline.} A rule engine with the ICAO
    transliteration tables, set-valued romanisation, MRZ check digits and
    cross-field temporal rules is at ceiling on clean input, which locates the
    contribution precisely: there is nothing to win on a clean scan.
  \item \textbf{A selective-prediction evaluation.} Comparing systems at their
    own operating points rewards abstention for free. Holding the automation
    error fixed instead shows that only the learned model can be tuned to an
    error budget at all, because only its confidence ranks correctness.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Natural language inference.}
Sentence-level NLI was popularised by large crowd-sourced corpora
\citep{bowman2015snli,williams2018mnli}, and document-level variants extend the
premise to long texts~\citep{yin2021docnli} and to contracts, where \neu likewise
marks a clause the document does not address~\citep{koreeda2021contractnli}.
Recent work incorporates implied content into the hypothesis
space~\citep{havaldar2025entailed} and extends NLI across documents and
languages~\citep{yuan2025cdcl}. Our premise is neither prose nor a single
document view: it is a set of field observations from several zones of one
document, in several scripts, each possibly corrupted.

\paragraph{Artefacts and minimal pairs.}
NLI models are known to exploit surface cues~\citep{gururangan2018annotation}
and syntactic heuristics~\citep{mccoy2019right}. Contrast sets evaluate a
model's local decision boundary with minimally edited
instances~\citep{gardner2020contrast}. We build every instance inside such a
group and score whole groups, so a system matching surface similarity rather
than inferring is exposed directly.

\paragraph{Transliteration and name matching.}
Machine transliteration is surveyed by \citet{syauqi2025advances}. The Dakshina
dataset~\citep{roark2020dakshina} supplies human-elicited romanisations for
South Asian languages, which we use to measure rather than assume ambiguity.
Fine-tuned language models have been applied to entity resolution in complex
linguistic contexts~\citep{chourasia2026sger}; we differ in grounding the match
in a document whose fields are themselves noisy.

\paragraph{Identity documents and OCR.}
Work on identity documents largely concerns classification and fraud:
document-type classification~\citep{voerman2025optimizing}, presentation-attack
detection~\citep{gonzalez2025forged} and forged-document
detection~\citep{munozharo2025fakeidet2}, with named-entity extraction for
document verification studied by \citet{toprak2023enhanced}. MIDV-2020
\citep{bulatov2022midv2020} is the standard image benchmark, but contains no
Devanagari, Tamil, Bengali or Perso-Arabic document, so it cannot calibrate four
of our five languages. LLM robustness to multilingual OCR noise has been
evaluated for question answering~\citep{piryani2025multiocrqa}.

\paragraph{Selective prediction.}
A classifier that may abstain is compared on a risk--coverage
curve~\citep{elyaniv2010foundations,geifman2017selective}. Validation pipelines
that defer uncertain cases to a person~\citep{tsaneva2025knowledge} face the same
trade-off. We report the deployment form of that curve: the review load needed
to meet a fixed error budget.

\section{The \kyc Task}
\label{sec:task}

\paragraph{Formulation.}
An instance is a triple $(P, h, y)$. The premise $P$ is the set of field
observations read from one document; each observation records a field (surname,
date of birth, \dots), the zone it was read from (VIZ or MRZ), the script, the
observed value \emph{after} OCR, and the OCR engine's confidence. The hypothesis
$h$ asserts one value for one field (``The holder's surname is
\textsc{Chatterjee}''). The label $y \in \{\ent, \con, \neu\}$.

\paragraph{\neu as evidence insufficiency.}
\neu holds when the document does not carry the evidence to settle $h$: the
field is absent from this document type, or every observation of it is
illegible. It is a property of the premise, not of a model's confidence, and
deployment maps it to human review. A system that treats \neu as ``low
confidence'' conflates two different reasons to defer.

\paragraph{Languages.}
We cover the five scripts of the Indian document context. English is the
control: every Indian document carries a Latin zone. Hindi, Tamil and Bengali
have no normative romanisation; Tamil does not mark voicing at all, so one
letter is read \textit{k} or \textit{g} by position. Urdu is written without
short vowels, so a consonant skeleton maps to several names, and it is the only
language with partial normative backing (the ICAO Arabic table).

\section{Building the Benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark}

\paragraph{Documents.}
For each language we fabricate identities from pools of
\emph{(native, attested Latin)} name pairs---the Latin form is attested, never
derived from our own transliterator---and issue each a TD3 passport. The MRZ is
built to ICAO Doc~9303 with real 7-3-1 check digits, validated character for
character against the standard's specimen. No byte derives from a real person.

\paragraph{Contrast groups.}
Each identity yields three groups that share one document and differ only in
the hypothesis: a \emph{cross-script name} group (an accepted alternative or the
ICAO romanisation, \ent; a different person's name chosen to be close in Latin
form, \con; a field the document does not carry, \neu), a \emph{temporal} group
(consistent dates; an impossible combination of issue, expiry and template era;
a date off by one day), and an \emph{evidence} group (an illegible field; an
absent field). Each instance is tagged with the phenomenon it probes.

\paragraph{Identity-level splitting.}
Because one identity produces several groups that share a surname, dates and a
document number, splitting by group leaks documents across the split. We split
by identity, stratified by language, and assert zero overlap. Table~\ref{tab:data}
gives the result.

\section{Measuring the Two Premises}
\label{sec:premises}

The argument rests on two empirical claims. We measure both
(Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}).

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig2_motivation}
  \caption{(a) Share of words with more than one human romanisation in Dakshina,
  with mean romanisations per word, over roughly 30,000 words per language.
  (b) Ratio of deletion to substitution rate. The hand-built noise model
  (grey) made substitution dominant; measured Tesseract output (violet) is the
  reverse at every level. Note the log scale.}
  \label{fig:motivation}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Romanisation is one-to-many.}
Over the Dakshina lexicons~\citep{roark2020dakshina}, the share of native words
with more than one human romanisation rises from 57.0\% in Hindi through 74.6\%
in Tamil and 90.2\% in Bengali to 97.6\% in Urdu, averaging 4.2 spellings per
Urdu word. The ordering was predicted from the scripts' properties before the
data was consulted. Dakshina also exposed the optimism of hand-written test
pairs: recall of our Urdu transliterator fell from 98\% on 40 pairs we had
written ourselves to 29.5\% on independent romanisations, and because Dakshina's
splits are word-disjoint, an attested-spelling lexicon gives no benefit on
unseen words.

\paragraph{OCR mostly deletes.}
MIDV-2020 cannot calibrate Indic or Perso-Arabic scripts, so we render known
text with HarfBuzz shaping and FreeType rasterisation, degrade it through five
capture conditions, read it with Tesseract~\citep{smith2007tesseract}, and align
the output to the input with a Levenshtein backtrace. Deletion exceeds
substitution at every level, by a factor of 1.3 to 3.6. A hand-built model of
the kind used in robustness studies had this backwards, with deletion rates 5
to 17 times too low. The distinction is not cosmetic: re-running the noise
ablation with the measured model moved the rule engine at moderate noise from
0.831 to 0.597 macro-F1. Rendered text has no paper grain, glare or perspective,
so these rates are a lower bound on real capture.

\section{Systems}
\label{sec:systems}

All three systems share one interface and are scored by one harness.

\paragraph{Exact match.}
Compares the claimed value with every observation of the field after case and
whitespace normalisation. It has no notion of \neu and never abstains---the
honest behaviour of the incumbent, and the source of its false-mismatch rate.

\paragraph{Rule engine.}
Built to win. It receives the ICAO tables, set-valued romanisation engines for
every script (an abugida traversal with script-specific alternations for the
Brahmic scripts; vowel readings of the Perso-Arabic \emph{matres lectionis} for
Urdu), MRZ check digits, multi-format date parsing and cross-field temporal
rules. A field is illegible below an OCR confidence of 0.35. Names match on the
best fuzzy similarity over every variant of every zone: at or above 88 is \ent,
at or below 60 is \con, and the band between is \neu. That band is the rule
engine's own version of conflating uncertainty with evidence absence, and we
retain it because removing it would weaken the baseline.

\paragraph{Cross-encoder.}
We fine-tune \texttt{distilbert-base-multilingual-cased}
\citep{sanh2019distilbert,devlin2019bert} as a three-way classifier over a
serialised premise and the hypothesis. Each observation is rendered as
\texttt{field:zone=value}, with values below the shared 0.35 threshold replaced
by an explicit unreadable marker; compressing the serialisation took the median
length from 347 to 159 subword tokens. The model never sees the pre-corruption
value, nor the transliteration variant sets---the rule engine's central
advantage---so any win over that baseline is a win on inference. With the
embedding table frozen (92M of 135M parameters), two epochs train in 136 minutes
on a four-thread CPU. The model is trained at moderate noise only.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

We report four levels, all mandatory: macro-F1 with per-class F1; per-language
macro-F1; per-phenomenon accuracy; and \emph{contrast-group consistency}, the
share of groups whose every member is correct, which a surface-matching system
cannot fake. Deployment rates follow: the false-mismatch rate (genuine holders
rejected) and the review load (share routed to a human).

Every macro-F1 carries a 95\% bootstrap interval~\citep{efron1993bootstrap} with
1,000 resamples drawn over \emph{contrast groups}, not instances: members of a
group share a premise, and treating them as independent narrows the interval by
about 7\%. Claims that one system beats another rest on a paired
bootstrap~\citep{koehn2004significance}.

\paragraph{Selective prediction.}
Systems abstain at different rates, and macro-F1 charges nothing for the human
labour abstention costs. We therefore also fix the automation error rate and
report the review load required to meet it. A \neu prediction counts as sent to
a human---never as an error, never as coverage---and a system that cannot meet a
budget at any threshold is reported as \emph{unreachable}, not as 100\% review.
The confidence threshold for each budget is chosen on the development split by
exact search over every achievable cut point, then applied unchanged to the test
split, and we report the test error it actually produces even when that exceeds
the budget. Thresholds tuned on the test split itself are used only for claims of
impossibility, for which they are the most generous setting available.

\paragraph{Main result.}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the held-out split at the noise level the model was
trained on. The cross-encoder reaches 0.972 macro-F1 against the rule engine's
0.601, and the paired bootstrap difference excludes zero. It also wins on the
metrics macro-F1 does not capture: contrast-group consistency rises from 0.307
to 0.936, genuine holders rejected fall from 9.3\% to 5.7\%, and review load
falls from 63.4\% to 28.6\%.

That last figure needs reading correctly. 28.6\% is exactly the share of \neu
instances in the split: the minimum review load any correct system can have. The
cross-encoder sits at that floor; the rule engine sends a further 35 percentage
points of \emph{decidable} cases to a human, abstaining on 48.8\% of cases the
document actually settles.

\paragraph{Robustness to capture quality.}
Because the generator's identity sampling is independent of the noise preset, we
can regenerate the identical 1,050 held-out instances---same identities, same
gold labels, same document numbers---at every noise level, varying only the OCR
damage (Table~\ref{tab:noise}, Figure~\ref{fig:noise}). On clean input the rule
engine is at ceiling (0.991) and statistically indistinguishable from the
cross-encoder (0.986): there is nothing to win on a clean scan. Under noise the
two diverge sharply. The rule engine falls to 0.928, 0.601 and 0.380; the
cross-encoder falls to 0.984, 0.972 and 0.911, holding up on heavy noise it was
never trained on. Its review load stays at the 28.6\% floor through moderate
noise and reaches 32.3\% at heavy, against the rule engine's 81.6\%.

\paragraph{The cost of an error budget.}
Figure~\ref{fig:selective}a plots review load against the error rate on the
cases each system decides alone. The rule engine already sends 63.4\% of traffic
to a human and is still wrong on 11.2\% of what it decides. With thresholds
chosen on development data it misses every budget on test: its 10\% setting
yields 11.2\% error, and its 5\% setting yields 5.2\% error while sending 92.7\%
of traffic to a human. Even a threshold tuned on the test split itself cannot
reach 5\%: an exhaustive sweep over all 37 of its distinct confidence values
bottoms out at 5.19\%. The cross-encoder meets the 10\% and 5\% budgets at its
default operating point (4.0\% error), meets a 2\% budget at 35.5\% review
(1.8\% test error), and at a 1\% budget reviews 41.5\% of traffic for a test
error of 1.1\%, narrowly over budget.

\paragraph{Why the rule engine cannot be tuned.}
Figure~\ref{fig:selective}b explains the gap. The cross-encoder's risk--coverage
curve is monotone and flat at zero error through 65\% coverage: its confidence
ranks correctness, so a threshold is a usable control. The rule engine's curve
is not monotone. Its confidences are constants attached to rules---0.99 for a
check-digit match, 0.85 for a literal mismatch, 0.5 for the indeterminate
band---so they record \emph{which rule fired}, not \emph{how likely that rule is
to be right}, and sorting by them sorts by rule. Exact match, which reports 1.0
everywhere, is flat at its overall error rate.

\paragraph{Where the errors are.}
Figure~\ref{fig:phenomena} breaks accuracy down by phenomenon. Every
cross-encoder error falls on the three cross-script name phenomena, hardest on
accepted alternative romanisations (0.839); all five structural phenomena are
perfect. A model that had learned only the temporal rules would show the
opposite pattern. The rule engine is perfect where the task is trivial---noticing
a missing or illegible field---and fails where its rules depend on reading
values that OCR has damaged: its temporal rules are correct, but once a date
such as \texttt{27~MAY~2028} reads \texttt{27~MY~20Z8} there is no date left to
reason about. By language (Table~\ref{tab:language}), Urdu is the weakest for
both systems, consistent with it being both the most one-to-many script and the
one Tesseract reads worst.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\paragraph{A leak that looked like a strong model.}
An earlier version of the split partitioned by contrast group. One identity
yields three groups sharing a surname, dates and a document number, so 359 of
383 test identities (94\%) also appeared in training, and the cross-encoder
scored 0.989. Every number from that run is discarded. The tell, in hindsight,
was \neu F1 of exactly 1.000 alongside uniformly high scores; the corrected split
lowers the headline to 0.972. We report this because the failure is general:
any generator that derives several instances from one latent entity leaks
through a split on anything finer than that entity.

\paragraph{\neu F1 of 1.000 is a property of the benchmark.}
The cross-encoder's \neu F1 is perfect, and so is the rule engine's on both
\neu phenomena. \neu instances are built by removing or destroying evidence,
which leaves a visible trace in the premise; detecting it is a surface pattern.
This confirms the generator implements the label definition, and nothing more.
A \neu class requiring genuine judgement needs human annotation.

\paragraph{The model generalises to heavier noise.}
Trained at moderate noise, the cross-encoder loses 0.075 macro-F1 between clean
and heavy noise, while the rule engine loses 0.610. The learned model has not
memorised a noise level; it has learned to recover a field from whichever zone
survived.

\section{Conclusion}

Posing identity verification as document-level inference with a structural
\neu turns review load into part of the task. On a benchmark built from measured
romanisation ambiguity and measured OCR noise, a strong rule engine is at
ceiling on clean input and collapses under realistic corruption, while a small
cross-encoder trained at one noise level holds up across all of them. The
decisive difference is not accuracy but calibration: only the learned model's
confidence ranks its own correctness, so only it can be operated at a fixed
error budget.

\section*{Limitations}

\textbf{Synthetic documents.} No real identity document is used, by design:
collecting them is not lawful. Romanisations and OCR rates are drawn from real
data, but the documents, names and dates are generated, and generated documents
are more regular than real ones.

\textbf{No human labels.} Gold labels follow from the generator's construction.
No annotator has labelled an instance, and no inter-annotator agreement is
reported. This is the most important limit on every number in this paper.

\textbf{Rendered, not photographed.} OCR rates are measured on rendered text
with simulated degradation; real captures add paper texture, glare, perspective
and wear. The rates are a lower bound, and only one OCR engine was measured.

\textbf{Urdu typography.} Urdu was rendered in a Naskh face, while Urdu
documents are commonly printed in Nastaliq, which OCR handles worse. Our Urdu
results are likely optimistic.

\textbf{Scale of the model.} The cross-encoder is a distilled multilingual model
trained on CPU with frozen embeddings, from a single seed. It is a floor, not a
ceiling; we report no variance across training runs.

\textbf{Point estimates on the selective curves.} Macro-F1 carries bootstrap
intervals; the operating points in Figure~\ref{fig:selective} do not.

\textbf{Structural \neu.} Because \neu is generated by removing evidence, the
benchmark does not test \neu cases that require judgement.

\section*{Ethics Statement}

The benchmark contains no personal data: every identity is fabricated, and
provenance records assert this for every instance. Automated identity
verification decides who can open a bank account, so its errors fall on people.
Exact matching rejects 89.3\% of genuine holders in our setting, and those
rejections are not evenly distributed: they concentrate on names whose
romanisation is most ambiguous. Both systems we study are weakest on Urdu, which
means holders with Urdu names would bear a disproportionate share of errors and
reviews. A deployed system should report error and review rates per script, and
its \neu cases must reach a human rather than default to rejection.
